title: 'The Spatial Cost of Formality: Street Vendors'' Preferences in India'
abstract: |
    Formalisation policies in developing countries have had mixed success, and their effects depend on design. Firms that operate on public land pose a distinct problem: the state cannot grant them legal status without also choosing where they may trade, so formality is offered at a location rather than at a price. We study this trade-off for street vendors, who account for 1.5 to 6.8% of employment in low- and middle-income countries and are commonly formalised through government-built vending zones. We embed a stated-preference experiment in an original geolocated survey of 2,922 vendors in 206 marketplaces in Patna, India, where the Street Vendors Act of 2014 makes certification free but ties legal protection to a vending zone. At an unchanged commute, the average vendor’s willingness to pay for a protected spot is 4.5% of monthly sales, and predicted take-up of a free spot is 68%. Willingness to pay falls to 2.1% of sales when the commute rises by 1 km and reaches zero at about 3.8 km; a 5 km move reduces take-up twice as much as a rent of 2% of sales. A randomised information treatment raises take-up by about 3 percentage points at every design. The results indicate that low take-up of vending zones reflects the relocation cost that place-based formalisation imposes, not low demand for formal status.
