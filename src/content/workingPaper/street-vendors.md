title: 'The Spatial Cost of Formality: Street Vendors'' Preferences in India'
abstract: |
    A state cannot grant legal status to a firm trading on public land without also deciding where it may trade. For these firms formality is offered at a location rather than at a price, and across the developing world it is delivered through designated vending zones that are commonly empty. We price what relocation costs such a firm, using a stated preferences experiment embedded in an original geo-located survey of 2,922 street vendors across 206 marketplaces in Patna, India, where the Street Vendors Act of 2014 makes certification free and conditions the legal right on location instead. Formality is valued: offered a zone at an unchanged commute, the average vendor would accept a rent of up to 4.5 per cent of monthly sales, and 69 per cent accept a free spot on those terms. What a zone is worth varies widely across vendors, and the 27 characteristics we observe shift where a vendor sits without explaining why vendors differ, so the ones a zone would reach cannot be identified in advance. Distance consumes that value and does so unevenly. The acceptable rent falls to 2.1 per cent of sales after a one kilometre longer commute and to 0.9 per cent after three, more than half the value going in the first kilometre alone, and reaches zero at about 3.8 kilometres, beyond which no rent is acceptable at all. Information raises the value of a zone, lifting take-up by about 3 percentage points from a baseline at which 1.3 per cent of vendors can name the Act, but changes none of these trade-offs. Zones sit empty not because the state has failed to offer formality or because vendors do not want it, but because conferring it requires moving the firm, and moving it costs most of what the legal status is worth.
