title: 'The Spatial Cost of Formality: Street Vendors'' Preferences in India'
abstract: |
    Formalisation policies in developing countries have proven hard to get right, and how much they achieve is sensitive to policy design. The problem is harder still for informal firms trading on public land, because a state cannot grant such a firm legal status without also deciding where it may trade. A large group of these firms is street vendors, who sell from carts, stalls and ground displays on streets and pavements and account for between 1.5 and 6.8 per cent of employment in low- and middle-income countries. States therefore offer them formality at a location rather than at a price, and deliver it through government-built vending zones that commonly stand empty. We price what relocation costs these firms, using a stated-preference experiment embedded in an original geolocated survey of 2,922 street vendors across 206 marketplaces in Patna, India, where the Street Vendors Act of 2014 makes certification free and attaches the protected spot to a location instead. Vendors value formality: at an unchanged commute the average vendor would pay up to 4.5 per cent of monthly sales for a zone, and 68 per cent accept one when the spot is free. Distance then consumes that value, and unevenly. The acceptable rent falls to 2.1 per cent of sales one kilometre further out and to nothing at about 3.8 kilometres, with more than half the value going in the first kilometre alone. Rent and information shift take-up less than distance does over the moves a zone is likely to require. Zones sit empty not because the state has failed to offer formality or because vendors do not want it, but because conferring it requires moving the firm, and moving it costs most of what it is worth.
